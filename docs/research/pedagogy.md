# Ear-training pedagogy for a "play songs by ear" app: research report

**Method and limits.** I opened every source below with WebFetch, and each claim links to the page it came from. The WebSearch budget ran out partway through. After that I found papers by following links on pages I had already opened, or by DOI lookup on Semantic Scholar. Where a claim rests on a secondary summary, the bullet says so. "Unverified" means I could not open an adequate source.

## 1. Functional (scale-degree) training vs. isolated-interval training

- **Benbassat's method (Functional Ear Trainer).** The app plays a short chord cadence to set the key, then a single tone, and the user names its scale degree. An advanced mode plays two notes, one after the other or together ([pract.is](https://pract.is/music-apps/functional-ear-trainer)). App descriptions then have the tone resolve to the tonic: lower tones descend, higher tones ascend. They say that changing octave or key "will not affect the recognition process" because the tone's function stays the same ([leadvocals.ca](https://www.leadvocals.ca/news/information-for-vocalists/improve-relative-pitch-with-functional-ear-trainer)). Benbassat's own site describes it only as a course "to recognize tones in a major and minor context" that "has proven to be very effective", with no data given ([miles.be](https://www.miles.be/software/functional-ear-trainer-v2)).
- **Related practitioner drills.**
  - The "Charlie Banacos exercise": a C–F–G7–C cadence, then random notes. More notes are added once accuracy reaches 75–90%. After a mistake, the learner moves "back and forth between the cadence and the note(s)" until it is clear ([miles.be/Singer](https://www.miles.be/articles/the-charlie-banacos-exercise/)).
  - A variant has the learner sing the note down to *do* by "the shortest stepwise diatonic path" in solfège ([miles.be](https://www.miles.be/articles/ear-training-a-direct-and-logical-path/)).
- **Bruce Arnold's "One Note" / Contextual Ear Training.**
  - A cadence, then one random note. The learner answers with "the first thing that comes into your mind" and moves up a level at about 80% correct, not 100% ([brucearnold.com](https://brucearnold.com/ear-training-guided-tour/)).
  - Each level doubles the speed. Practice is 5–10 minutes, several times a day ([muse-eek](https://muse-eek.com/ear-training-one-note-for-musicians-book-with-audio/)).
  - He advises *against* resolving to the tonic "even as a[n] initial step", because "it takes too much time… as music is going by in real time" ([muse-eek FAQ](https://muse-eek.com/faq/avoiding-resolving-to-the-tonic-during-ear-training/)).
  - So the two main practitioner methods agree on cadence first and scale-degree identity, but disagree about resolving to the tonic.
- **Kodály movable-do.**
  - Syllables name a note's function in the key, with hand signs. Children meet a concept by listening, singing or moving before they learn to notate it. Teaching starts from the pentatonic scale: so–mi, then la, do and re, and only then fa and ti ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kod%C3%A1ly_method)).
  - In a survey of 270 US college theory instructors, most chose pitch systems that emphasize scale-degree function ([Paney & Buonviri 2017](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/8755123316686815)).
- **The case against intervals out of context.**
  - Karpinski (2000), quoted by Chenette: "a preponderance of experimental evidence shows little connection between the ability to identify intervals acontextually and the ability to do so in a tonal context". Chenette adds, citing Huron, that scale degree rather than interval is probably how the brain encodes music ([Chenette 2021](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.21.27.2/mto.21.27.2.chenette.html)). Another page traces the finding to Shatzkin (1981), *JRME* 29(2) ([StringKick](https://www.stringkick.com/blog-lessons/interval-ear-training/)).
  - Reference-song associations are "applicable only to the specific scale-degrees found in each melody" (Rogers 1983) ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interval_recognition)).
  - Telesco: "intervals have no meaning outside of the context of a harmony and/or key" ([miles.be/Telesco](https://www.miles.be/articles/contextual-ear-training/)).
- **Context changes how intervals are heard.**
  - Shatzkin (1984), 36 music majors: a tone *before* an interval made them overestimate its size, and a tone *after* made them underestimate it. He proposed a "tonic effect" ([SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.2307/3345276)).
  - Graves & Oxenham (2017): a familiar diatonic context improved interval-size discrimination, but only modestly (d′ gain about 0.47 melodic, 0.28 harmonic). They put it down mostly to "priming of expected stimuli" ([Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.01753/full)).
  - Weaknesses of functional hearing: it fails when there is no tonic or the tonality is ambiguous, and after a key change the listener has to re-orient ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ear_training)).
- **Counter-evidence and nuance.**
  - Nichols & Springer (2022), n=35: a separate interval-identification test explained 28.9% of the variance in melodic-dictation scores after controlling for experience ([SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/00224294211011962)).
  - Hung (2012), 85 students: those trained in fixed-do sight-sang more accurately than those trained in movable-do, at every level of complexity. The groups came from prior training and were not randomized ([USF](https://repository.usfca.edu/diss/38/)).
  - When learning intervals without reference songs or singing, interleaving interval types beat blocking. With those aids, the two were equal ([Wong, Chen & Lim 2021](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0305735620922595)).
- **Gap (unverified, not found).** I found no controlled study that compares cadence-plus-scale-degree training with isolated-interval training on real-melody recognition or playback. The case for the functional approach rests on expert argument plus indirect and correlational evidence.

## 2. Karpinski: stages of melodic dictation, memory, hearings, difficulty

- **Four stages that loop:** hearing (attention plus perception), short-term melodic memory, musical understanding (labelling what was remembered), and notation ([Chenette 2018](https://digitalcollections.lipscomb.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1190&context=jmtp); [Baker 2019](https://davidjohnbaker1.github.io/document/intro.html)). Karpinski's own 2019 webinar slide reads: "Focused attention → Short-term melodic memory → Understanding → Notation" ([Brown & Karpinski](https://peabody.jhu.edu/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/Ear-Training-Online.pdf)).
- **Memory can be stretched only two ways.** *Extractive listening*: focusing on one part and memorizing it "despite sonic interference". *Chunking*: using mental schemata so that more notes fit into each memory slot ([Chenette 2018](https://digitalcollections.lipscomb.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1190&context=jmtp)). Karpinski proposes separate encodings for contour and for pitch ([Baker](https://davidjohnbaker1.github.io/document/intro.html)). Working memory holds about 3–4 chunks (Cowan) and "may be fixed by adulthood", so training should aim at using it efficiently ([Chenette 2021](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.21.27.2/mto.21.27.2.chenette.html)).
- **Hearings and length.**
  - Baker reports Karpinski (2000) as saying that two hearings "should be adequate for a listener with few to no chunking skills to be able to dictate a melody of twelve to twenty notes". This wording is as Baker reports it; I could not check it against the book, so treat it as partly unverified ([Baker](https://davidjohnbaker1.github.io/document/intro.html)).
  - Pembrook: a second hearing gave a "dramatic increase" in scores, and melodic memory capacity is about 7–11 notes. Both come via Paney's review, pp. 11 and 30 ([Paney 2007](https://ttu-ir.tdl.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/688ad13a-e2b0-41fc-953b-5a8c089644a4/content)).
  - Typical research protocols play a melody 4 times with 30 s gaps ([Nichols & Springer 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12341474/)) or 5 times with 20 s gaps ([Baker ch. 6](https://davidjohnbaker1.github.io/document/experiment.html)).
- **Dictation vs. transcription.** Dictation means a fixed number of playings, a time limit after each, and the whole excerpt each time. Transcription means unlimited playings with stop and start. Brown & Karpinski warn: "Transcription Can Become a Pitch-Matching Exercise" ([webinar](https://peabody.jhu.edu/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/Ear-Training-Online.pdf)).
- **No scaffolding before the melody.** Karpinski (2017, pp. x–xi, quoted by Guez): instructors should "not play anything before students hear a dictation… no chord progression, no scale, not even a starting pitch". Students should work out the tonic, starting degree, pulse and meter themselves, and "we should only reveal those facts that are not audible" (1990) ([Guez 2022](https://digitalcollections.lipscomb.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1407&context=jmtp)).
- **How difficulty progresses.**
  - Karpinski's pitch patterns start stepwise and settle the tonic triad. Minor mode is a separate topic ([Lovell 2019](https://digitalcollections.lipscomb.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1203&context=jmtp)).
  - Guez's real-recording templates give key and meter at first and later make students determine them. Excerpts then get longer and textures denser, and chromaticism comes last ([Guez 2022](https://digitalcollections.lipscomb.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1407&context=jmtp); [Guez 2019](https://digitalcollections.lipscomb.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1379&context=jmtp)).
  - Ortmann (via Paney): repeated notes are missed less often, fewer changes of direction and ascending lines are easier, and larger intervals are harder ([Paney 2007](https://ttu-ir.tdl.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/688ad13a-e2b0-41fc-953b-5a8c089644a4/content)).
  - Note density had a large effect on difficulty, and tonalness a significant one ([Baker ch. 6](https://davidjohnbaker1.github.io/document/experiment.html)).
  - Karpinski's own guidance on range and chromaticism is **unverified**: the book is not online.
- **What predicts dictation skill.** Tonal working memory (β=.41), years of piano lessons (β=.41, even for non-pianists) and semesters of aural-skills classes (β=.35) together explained 36.5% of the variance ([Nichols & Springer 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12341474/)). Talking students through a dictation step by step *lowered* their scores (n=64) ([Paney 2016](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0305735614547409)).
- **Chenette's reframing.** Treat extractive listening as a skill: "selectively remember and transcribe either the first half or the second half of a melody that is too long to remember in its entirety" ([Chenette 2021](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.21.27.2/mto.21.27.2.chenette.html)). He also proposes working-memory drills: change detection, n-back humming, melody updating and running span ([Chenette 2018](https://digitalcollections.lipscomb.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1190&context=jmtp)).

## 3. Gordon's Music Learning Theory, audiation, and singing

- **Audiation** "takes place when we hear and comprehend music for which the sound is no longer or may never have been present". Its listening stages include establishing tonality and meter and predicting patterns ([GIML](https://giml.org/mlt/audiation/)). Gordon: "audiation is to music what thought is to language" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gordon_music_learning_theory)).
- **Skill Learning Sequence.** Discrimination learning comes first: aural/oral, then verbal association, partial synthesis, symbolic association and composite synthesis. Inference learning follows: generalization, creativity/improvisation, then theory ([GIML](https://giml.org/mlt/skilllearningsequence/)). Patterns are sung on neutral syllables first "to hear the pure sound", then given solfège names, and only then notation ([Improving Musician](https://theimprovingmusician.com/gordons-skill-learning-sequence-the-30000-foot-view/)).
- **Tonal and rhythm patterns are taught separately.** "Tonal patterns are taught in a context free of rhythm; likewise, rhythm patterns are chanted", because combining them "significantly compounds the complexity". Pattern work takes about 5–8 minutes per class ([GIML FAQ](https://giml.org/resources/faq/)).
- **Evidence for vocal pattern work.** In a randomized study of 48 beginning instrumentalists, tonal patterns taught through harmonization and vocalization led to significantly better identification of major vs. minor tonality and better melodic sight-reading than a notation-first course. Reading recognition did not differ ([Grutzmacher 1987](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.2307/3344959)).
- **Singing *during* dictation tends to hurt.**
  - Singing a preparatory solfège pattern lowered scores ([Buonviri 2015](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0022429415570754)).
  - Being required to sing before notating lowered scores. Only 18% sang every melody correctly, and singing accuracy correlated with scores ([Buonviri 2019](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0022429418801333)).
  - Singing hindered students, especially less experienced ones, in a study of 68 ([Lima et al. 2021](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0255761421989114)).
  - Pembrook: "most people cannot sing accurately enough after one hearing to benefit" (via [Paney 2007](https://ttu-ir.tdl.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/688ad13a-e2b0-41fc-953b-5a8c089644a4/content)).
  - The exception: group sight-singing *before* dictation helped in a pilot with 54 students ([Caregnato et al. 2024](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/02557614231152306)).
- **Singing helps when playing by ear.** Singing a melody back took fewer trials than playing it on an instrument ([Woody & Lehmann 2010](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ889094)). Popular musicians learning from recordings sang or hummed to set "goal tones" or "to hold them in memory" ([Liscio & Brown 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.04058v1)). A jazz practice site: "Singing is your first test… it shows you right away if you're actually hearing the pitches or just guessing" ([Jazzadvice](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/10-killer-tips-for-transcribing-jazz-solos/)).
- **The voice itself is a limit.**
  - Nonmusicians matched pitch far better with a slider than with their voice (about 13 vs. 38 cents mean error). Five of them (23%) were poor vocal matchers, off by more than 50 cents ([Hutchins et al. 2014](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13414-014-0732-1)).
  - Training pitch discrimination alone did not improve singing accuracy ([Zarate et al. 2010](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0011181)).
  - Practising one interval consistently, whether by listening or singing, transferred better to singing a new interval than varied practice ([Harvey et al. 1987](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0305735687151007)).
- **Synthesis (my reading).** The evidence supports vocal pattern work and "sing, then find" as practice tools. It does not show that singing is *the* key, and forcing singing at the moment of answering tends to hurt.

## 4. Melody memory, and playing by ear as a skill

- **Contour and scale.**
  - Dowling (1978): melodies are stored as an overlearned scale schema plus a contour. Listeners confuse "tonal answers" (the same contour shifted along the same scale) with exact transpositions ([Dowling 1978](https://bpb-us-e2.wpmucdn.com/labs.utdallas.edu/dist/f/100/files/2021/03/1978-2.pdf)).
  - Untransposed melodies are recognized by their exact pitches. Transposed *novel* melodies are recognized mainly by contour. For familiar tunes, distortions that kept contour plus relative interval sizes were recognized 66% of the time, contour only 59%, and harmony only 28% ([Dowling & Fujitani 1971](https://www.brainmusic.org/EducationalActivities/DowlingFujitani_1971.pdf)).
- **Over time, detail takes over.** Contour dominates after 5 s, interval information after longer delays. Interval information is "difficult to encode" but "retained with high efficiency" ([Dowling & Bartlett 1981](https://bpb-us-e2.wpmucdn.com/labs.utdallas.edu/dist/f/100/files/2021/03/1981.pdf)). This shift happens only for tonal melodies ([Dowling 1991](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03212222)).
- **Capacity and tonality.**
  - 7-tone series were recognized better than 10-tone ones, and strongly tonal series better than weakly tonal ones. Contour complexity had no effect ([Croonen 1994](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03211677)).
  - Musicians' memory advantage is large for tonal material (meta-analysis of 29 studies) ([Talamini et al. 2017](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0186773)).
  - Short-term memory for tones has limited capacity, as verbal memory does ([Williamson et al. 2010](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/MC.38.2.163)).
- **Chunking into motifs.** Rhythmic grouping "determines subjective chunking", so test items that crossed group boundaries were harder ([Dowling 1973](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03198614)). Note sequences made of labelled tonal cells (triads, cadences) were recalled better, especially longer lists of 8–9 notes ([Lörch 2022](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/03057356211013396)).
- **Transposition.** How similar a transposition sounds depends mainly on how far it moved in pitch and only slightly on key distance ([Van Egmond et al. 1996](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03207557)). Decoy melodies in the same or a nearby key caused more false alarms, and this held regardless of age or experience ([Bartlett & Dowling 1980](https://bpb-us-e2.wpmucdn.com/labs.utdallas.edu/dist/f/100/files/2021/03/1980.pdf)).
- **Lucy Green (informal learning).**
  - Learners choose the music, copy it by ear, learn alone and with friends, and pick up skills "in haphazard, idiosyncratic and holistic ways, starting with 'whole,' 'real-world' pieces" (summary by [Narita 2018](https://act.maydaygroup.org/volume-17-issue-3/act-17-3-57-78/)). Recordings are the "principal, aural means of… skill-acquisition" rather than a simple-to-complex progression ([Green 2005](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1479473/1/Green2005Music27.pdf)).
  - The book's sample was 14 musicians ([Allsup 2008](https://www.tc.columbia.edu/faculty/rea10/faculty-profile/files/inganEducationalFrameworkforPopularMusic2008.pdf)). I did not open the book itself.
  - Ear Playing Project, 75 students copying riffs from recordings: four learning styles appeared (impulsive, practical, shot-in-the-dark, theoretical). The most common strategy was "listening without playing" ([Varvarigou & Green](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/10103253/8/Green_Varvarigou%2C%20M%2C%20and%20Green%2C%20L%2C%20Learning%20styles%20and%20strats%20D16%2C%20MARCH%2014.pdf)). 93% of teachers felt students benefited ([IOE briefing](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1479500/1/RB19_Playing_by_Ear_in_the_Instrumental_Lesson_Green.pdf)).
- **Woody and McPherson.**
  - Musicians with vernacular (pop/folk) experience needed fewer trials. Classically trained musicians spent attention on fingerings ([Woody & Lehmann 2010](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ889094)).
  - A printed chord chart helped mid-level ear players make their mental image of the melody "more harmonically substantive" ([Woody 2020](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0305735618816365)).
  - Ear playing supports improvising, sight-reading and playing from memory ([Woody 2012](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ987231)).
  - McPherson (2005), 157 children over 3 years: strategy explained 71% of the variance in playing by ear by year 3. The best children "mentally rehearsed the music either by playing along with the recording or in the gaps". Early weakness in ear playing was associated with dropping out ([McPherson 2005](https://www.academia.edu/24184665/From_child_to_musician_skill_development_during_the_beginning_stages_of_learning_an_instrument)). Details of his 1997 path model are **unverified**: only the abstract was available ([ERIC](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ549911&pg=7237&q=questionnaire)).
- **How people actually transcribe.**
  - Across 18 YouTube videos, only 4 showed slow-down or loop features being used. Most learners worked on riffs, solos or a subset of chords rather than whole songs ([Liscio & Brown 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.04058v1)).
  - Jazz practice advice: sing first, work one phrase at a time, slow down and loop with software, then memorize and transpose to all 12 keys ([Jazzadvice](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/10-killer-tips-for-transcribing-jazz-solos/)).

## 5. Absolute pitch vs. relative pitch

- **Van Hedger et al. 2019:** 6 adults trained about 32 hours over 8 weeks. 2 of 6 reached "genuine" absolute-pitch (AP) criteria, and the best learner kept it for 4 or more months ([PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0223047)).
- **Wong et al. 2020 (*AP&P*):** 43 adults did 12–40 hours of "gamified and personalized" training, with the number of pitches raised from 3 to 12. 14% (6 of 43) named all 12 at 90% or better. Generalization depended on the training stimuli, and gains lasted 1–3 months ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13414-019-01869-3)). A summary of the same work: [Psychonomic](https://featuredcontent.psychonomic.org/5220-2/).
- **Wong et al. 2020 (*QJEP*):** 13 speakers of non-tonal languages, 20 hours. Two learned all 12 pitches, and relative pitch was unchanged ([SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1747021820935776)).
- **Who succeeds:** auditory working-memory capacity predicts how well adults learn AP ([Van Hedger et al. 2015](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0010027715000621)).
- **Review (2025):** prevalence estimates range from under 1% to 65% depending on method. AP "in most studies cannot be reliably trained", while relative pitch "is a necessary skill for musicians and can be developed through practice" ([Bairnsfather et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11750914/)).
- **AP and dictation:** AP correlated with dictation scores at r=.81 among 60 musicians. This is correlational only ([Dooley & Deutsch 2010](http://dooleykevin.com/Dooley.Deutsch.2010.pdf)). Miyazaki's claim that AP interferes with interval tasks was disputed by Dooley & Deutsch (2011). I saw that only on [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_pitch); the primary papers were not opened.
- **Practical conclusion (my reading).** A minority of adults can learn AP, and it takes tens of hours. Playing songs by ear needs relative, functional pitch. In the Ear Playing Project, the 10 students who seemed to have AP were spread across all four learning styles, which is weak evidence of no advantage ([Varvarigou & Green](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/10103253/8/Green_Varvarigou%2C%20M%2C%20and%20Green%2C%20L%2C%20Learning%20styles%20and%20strats%20D16%2C%20MARCH%2014.pdf)). AP should not be the app's core promise.

## 6. Rhythm and harmony pedagogy

- **Rhythm separately first, then combined.** Gordon chants rhythm patterns on their own ([GIML FAQ](https://giml.org/resources/faq/)). Guez's curriculum begins with a rhythm-only unit (simple meter, then compound, then several parts) before pitch. Karpinski, though, prefers to "contextualize rhythmic listening… as part of the broader study of melodic dictation" ([Guez 2022](https://digitalcollections.lipscomb.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1407&context=jmtp)).
- **Takadimi rhythm syllables.** Syllables are named by position within the beat. Teaching goes from sound to spoken description before notation, uses "a relatively small number of patterns", and takes simple meter before compound ([Hoffman, Pelto & White 1996](https://takadimi.net/documents/TakadimiArticle.pdf)).
- **Where rhythm gets hard.** Instructors report that compound meter is difficult ([Paney & Buonviri 2017](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/8755123316686815)), and teachers report that students struggle to notate rhythm ([Paney & Buonviri 2014](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0022429413508411)). Attending to rhythm first "may be an effective way of maximizing rhythmic accuracy" (Beckett 1997, via [Paney 2007](https://ttu-ir.tdl.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/688ad13a-e2b0-41fc-953b-5a8c089644a4/content)). Rhythmic grouping also sets memory chunks ([Dowling 1973](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03198614)).
- **Harmony: the bass line first.**
  - Chenette proposes "bass-line dictation alone", singing the bass back, and describing cadences ([Chenette 2021](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.21.27.2/mto.21.27.2.chenette.html)).
  - One textbook's order: chord colours, then the bass ("the strongest predictor of chord quality and inversion"), then the soprano ([Integrated Aural Skills](https://uidaho.pressbooks.pub/auralskills/chapter/ear-training-what-to-expect-in-harmonic-dictation/)).
  - "Often the bass note alone is enough… to identify the whole chord". Start with I–IV–V and sing the bass lines of known songs ([Musical U](https://www.musical-u.com/learn/hearing-chord-progressions-i-iv-v-in-major-keys/)).
- **The Do/Ti test.** Listeners hold do or ti as a guide tone to sort chords, then judge quality and function, and only then the bass and inversion ([Stevens 2016](https://digitalcollections.lipscomb.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1185&context=jmtp)).
- **What pop harmony is made of.**
  - In 100 canonical rock songs, the chords were I 32.8%, IV 22.6%, V 16.3%, ♭VII 8.1% and VI 7.2%. These five cover 87% of all harmonies. IV is the chord most often before I, and IV–V–I, V–IV–I and ♭VII–IV–I are the most frequent endings ([de Clercq & Temperley 2011](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/declercq-temperley-pm11.pdf)).
  - Across 1,300 songs: I, IV and V are most common, then vi ([Hooktheory](https://www.hooktheory.com/blog/i-analyzed-the-chords-of-1300-popular-songs-for-patterns-this-is-what-i-found/)).
- **Four-chord schemas.** Doo-wop I–vi–IV–V; singer/songwriter vi–IV–I–V or I–V–vi–IV, which is tonally ambiguous; hopscotch IV–V–vi–I. They are best told apart by "how the major tonic is approached" ([Open Music Theory](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/4-chord-schemas/)). The rotations of I–V–vi–IV are listed on [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I%E2%80%93V%E2%80%93vi%E2%80%93IV_progression).
- **What "hearing chords" means for a pop player (synthesis).** It means hearing root motion in the bass and a small set of functions (I, IV, V, vi, ♭VII) and schemas relative to the tonic. That harmonic picture also helps with picking out the melody ([Woody 2020](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0305735618816365)).

## 7. Adaptive training and target success rates

- **Staircases.** Transformed up-down rules settle at fixed accuracy levels: 2-down/1-up at 70.7%, 3-down/1-up at 79.4% ([Levitt 1971](https://www.academia.edu/81603738/Transformed_Up_Down_Methods_in_Psychoacoustics)). Later rules cover 5% steps ([Brown 1996](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03205497)).
- **The 85% rule.** For gradient-descent learners on two-choice tasks, the best training error is about 15.87%, i.e. about 85% correct. The authors caution that "our assumptions may not always be met" in more complex tasks ([Wilson et al. 2019](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-019-12552-4)). Practitioner thresholds are close: Arnold's 80% and Banacos's 75–90% (links in §1).
- **Engagement vs. learning.** In experiments with 10,000 and 70,000 players, players stayed longer when the game was easier, but "the most engaging design conditions produced the slowest rates of learning" ([Lomas et al. 2013](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1145/2470654.2470668?fields=title,abstract,year,authors,venue)).
- **Duolingo's Birdbrain.** It predicts each learner's chance of success on each exercise to keep them "engaged but… learning at the edge of their abilities", and it raised both engagement and learning in controlled tests. No target percentage is published, so any specific figure is unverified ([IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/duolingo)).
- **Session scheduling.** On an interval-comparison task, alternating practice with listen-only exposure took accuracy from about 69% to 88%. Continuous practice with the same number of presentations produced no learning ([Little et al. 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13414-018-1584-x)). Interleaving beats blocking, yet learners wrongly believe blocking works better ([Wong et al. 2021](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0305735620922595)). Adding categories gradually, as in AP training's 3→12 pitches, is one proven design ([Wong et al. 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13414-019-01869-3)).
- **Transfer is a warning.**
  - Computer auditory training for hearing loss improved the trained tasks (9 of 10 studies), but gains on untrained tasks were "small and not robust" ([Henshaw & Ferguson 2013](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0062836)).
  - A meta-analysis of Fast ForWord (adaptive auditory training for children's language and reading) found "no significant effect… on any outcome measure" ([Strong et al. 2011](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1111/j.1469-7610.2010.02329.x?fields=title,abstract,year,authors,venue); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fast_ForWord)).
- **Session length.** Practitioners recommend short, frequent sessions: 5–10 minutes several times a day ([Arnold](https://muse-eek.com/ear-training-one-note-for-musicians-book-with-audio/)), 10 minutes a day ([Functional Ear Trainer](https://getmusictools.com/functional-ear-trainer)), or 5–8 minutes of pattern work per class ([Gordon](https://giml.org/resources/faq/)).

## Design implications

These are my recommendations, built on the findings above. The links point to the evidence each one rests on.

1. **Set the key first, then take the support away.** Early items start with a cadence, and the keyboard shows scale degrees (1–7 or do–ti) relative to that key. Later levels cut back to a tonic drone, then to nothing, so the learner must find *do* from the melody, as with a real song ([Guez/Karpinski](https://digitalcollections.lipscomb.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1407&context=jmtp)). Add a separate "find the tonic" exercise ([Chenette](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.21.27.2/mto.21.27.2.chenette.html)).
2. **Start with one note.** The on-ramp is a cadence plus one note, and the learner names the degree. Begin with a few degrees and add more gradually ([Wong 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13414-019-01869-3)). Playing the resolution to the tonic should be optional feedback that fades, not a required step, because experts disagree on it ([leadvocals](https://www.leadvocals.ca/news/information-for-vocalists/improve-relative-pitch-with-functional-ear-trainer) vs. [Arnold](https://muse-eek.com/faq/avoiding-resolving-to-the-tonic-during-ear-training/)).
3. **Pick a random key and register for every item.** A repeated key lets learners lean on exact pitches instead of relationships ([Dowling & Fujitani](https://www.brainmusic.org/EducationalActivities/DowlingFujitani_1971.pdf)).
4. **Separate the scored drill from free exploration.** Scored mode gives a fixed number of hearings (start at 3–4 and fade to 2), a silent keyboard while answering, and one committed answer. Free mode allows unlimited replay and sounding keys, but it should not move the difficulty ladder, because otherwise the task becomes pitch matching ([Brown & Karpinski](https://peabody.jhu.edu/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/Ear-Training-Online.pdf)).
5. **Generate tonal, chunkable melodies, not random notes.** Use stepwise and triad cells, motif repetition and rhythmic grouping, and end phrases on stable degrees ([Lörch](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/03057356211013396); [Croonen](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03211677); [Dowling 1973](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03198614)).
6. **Make each difficulty dimension its own ladder parameter.** Dimensions: length (3→5→7→about 10 notes, then two-phrase melodies); note density and tempo; leap size and number of direction changes; degree set (pentatonic, then diatonic, then chromatic); major before minor as a separate track. Change one dimension at a time ([Baker](https://davidjohnbaker1.github.io/document/experiment.html); [Paney 2007/Ortmann](https://ttu-ir.tdl.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/688ad13a-e2b0-41fc-953b-5a8c089644a4/content); [Kodály](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kod%C3%A1ly_method); [Lovell](https://digitalcollections.lipscomb.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1203&context=jmtp)).
7. **Add partial-listening tasks for melodies too long to hold.** For example: play back only the last 4 notes, only the first phrase, or say which degree it ends on ([Chenette 2021](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.21.27.2/mto.21.27.2.chenette.html)).
8. **Grade contour and exact degrees separately.** Give partial credit for a correct up/down shape, since contour is learned before intervals ([Dowling & Bartlett](https://bpb-us-e2.wpmucdn.com/labs.utdallas.edu/dist/f/100/files/2021/03/1981.pdf)). After an error, replay the target and the learner's answer in context ([Banacos](https://www.miles.be/articles/the-charlie-banacos-exercise/)).
9. **Offer "hum it, then find it", but never force singing while answering.** If you add microphone pitch detection, grade leniently and never block progress: about 1 in 4 nonmusicians match pitch poorly, and forced singing mid-dictation lowers scores ([Hutchins](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13414-014-0732-1); [Buonviri 2019](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0022429418801333); [Woody & Lehmann](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ889094)).
10. **Keep rhythm separate at first.** Tap-back rhythms on a single pitch, and pitch-only items in even notes; combine them later. Simple meter comes before compound ([GIML](https://giml.org/resources/faq/); [Takadimi](https://takadimi.net/documents/TakadimiArticle.pdf)).
11. **Adapt each skill toward about 80% correct.** A 3-down/1-up staircase settles near 79% ([Levitt](https://www.academia.edu/81603738/Transformed_Up_Down_Methods_in_Psychoacoustics)). Open sessions with an easy warm-up and end on a success so streaks stay achievable, but keep the at-the-edge items in the middle, because easier feels better yet learns slower ([Lomas](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1145/2470654.2470668?fields=title,abstract,year,authors,venue)). Track a per-degree confusion table to decide what to serve next ([Birdbrain](https://spectrum.ieee.org/duolingo)).
12. **Short, mixed sessions with listen-only breaks.** Aim for 5–10 minutes a day, mix item types rather than drilling one, and add passive listening rounds between trials ([Little 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13414-018-1584-x); [Wong 2021](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0305735620922595)).
13. **Connect to real songs early.** Give familiar or public-domain tunes in random keys: find the tonic, hum, then find the notes. Later, allow imported audio with loop and slow-down, and focus on riffs and phrases rather than whole songs ([Green/Narita](https://act.maydaygroup.org/volume-17-issue-3/act-17-3-57-78/); [Liscio & Brown](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.04058v1)).
14. **Harmony path:** sing or play the bass root first; then I, IV and V, then vi and ♭VII; then the four-chord schemas, told apart by how they reach the tonic; then melody over a displayed chord chart; chord-quality drills last ([de Clercq & Temperley](https://davidtemperley.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/declercq-temperley-pm11.pdf); [Open Music Theory](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/4-chord-schemas/); [Woody 2020](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0305735618816365)).
15. **Promise relative pitch, and measure whether it carries over.** Do not market absolute pitch (2 of 6 and 6 of 43 learners succeeded: [Van Hedger](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0223047), [Wong](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13414-019-01869-3)). Periodically test on untrained, real melodies, because in-app gains often don't generalize ([Henshaw & Ferguson](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0062836)).
